The first thing my drawing teacher told me was to stop looking at my hand. This sounds counterintuitive — surely, drawing requires watching what you are drawing with — but she was right. The hand does not need supervision. What it needs is to translate what the eye is doing into line. Looking at the hand interrupts this transmission. You draw what you think you see rather than what you actually see.

I was fifty-one years old and had not drawn anything since a high school art class I had largely spent flirting with the person at the adjacent desk. I had come to drawing not from ambition or dissatisfaction but from a crisis of the kind that forces a reckoning with how you have been spending your time. My father had died. The question of how to spend a life, which I had been successfully postponing for decades, arrived with sudden urgency. I signed up for an evening drawing class in a church basement and showed up with the wrong kind of pencils.

## The Humiliation of Incompetence

Learning something as an adult is a specific kind of experience. As a child, incompetence is the expected condition. Everyone around you is also incompetent; no one expects otherwise. The embarrassment of not knowing how to do things is low because there is no established self to humiliate.

As an adult, the calculation is different. You have a self. That self has an established relationship with competence — you are good at some things, adequate at others, and you have generally arranged your life to avoid extended encounters with your inadequacies. Learning something from scratch, in which you must be bad for a long time before you become less bad, is a confrontation with the self in an unusual and not always pleasant way.

My drawings in the first weeks were bad. Not charmingly naive or interestingly awkward. Just bad. The proportions were wrong. The lines were either timid or wild, never right. Objects that I understood perfectly well in three dimensions became mysterious and wrong when I tried to render them in two. My teacher, who was kind in the way that requires effort to sustain, said things like "You're overcomplicating it" and "Stop thinking."

Stopping thinking turned out to be the hard part.

## Seeing Versus Knowing

What drawing teaches, if you let it, is a distinction between seeing and knowing. We think we see the world as it is. We do not. We see a heavily edited interpretation of the world, organized by concept and category, that allows us to function efficiently without having to process every perceptual datum from scratch. This editing is mostly useful. It becomes a problem when you try to draw.

You know what a hand looks like. You know it has five fingers, a palm, a thumb that is set apart from the rest. When you draw a hand from memory, you draw your concept of a hand. The result looks like a hand the way a stick figure looks like a person — correct in category but wrong in everything specific.

Drawing requires something different: actually looking at the hand in front of you, which is not a concept but a specific arrangement of forms in specific light at a specific moment. The light is falling this way today. This finger is foreshortened because of the angle. The thumbnail is catching a highlight here, not here. None of this was in my concept of a hand.

The same principle extends beyond drawing. We walk through environments and see only our categories — shop, street, person, car. The drawing student who has spent three months actually looking at things sees something different: the specific way this particular afternoon light hits this particular façade; the exact color, which is not "white" or "gray" but something more specific than either. Drawing does not make you a better artist only. It makes you a better seer.

## What Difficulty Is For

About four months in, something changed. Not dramatically — there was no breakthrough moment, no sudden competence. But I began to be able to tell, sometimes, when a drawing was going wrong, and occasionally to correct it. The feedback loop between eye, hand, and line began to function. I started to look forward to the evenings in the church basement.

The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's concept of flow — the state of absorbed engagement in which self-consciousness drops away and activity becomes intrinsically rewarding — is usually described in relation to activities people are already skilled at. But something like it is also available, I found, in the focused struggle of learning. Not the frustration of the first weeks, which was merely unpleasant, but the engaged difficulty of the middle months, when you can feel yourself improving and the problem is just at the edge of your ability.

This is not a comfortable state. It requires tolerance for difficulty and failure that adults have typically stopped cultivating. But it is, I think, underrated as a source of something important — engagement with experience in a way that is resistant to habit and distraction, that requires your full attention and returns it to you as a kind of aliveness.

My drawings are still not good in any objective sense. They are, in the charitable light of people who care about me, "getting there." But getting there is, it turns out, a perfectly acceptable destination. The point was never to arrive.
