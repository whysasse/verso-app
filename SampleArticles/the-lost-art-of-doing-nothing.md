The Dutch have a word for it: *niksen*. It means, roughly, to do nothing — not meditating, not exercising, not being productive in any measurable sense. Just sitting. Looking out a window. Letting your mind drift without destination.

It sounds easy. It is, in practice, one of the hardest things many of us have ever tried to do.

## The Guilt of Idleness

There is a particular guilt that attaches itself to stillness in modern life. We have internalized the logic of productivity so completely that an afternoon without output feels like theft — from our employers, our families, ourselves. Even our leisure has become optimized: we read books that will make us smarter, exercise in ways that are scientifically validated, meditate on apps that track our streaks.

The philosopher Bertrand Russell wrote an essay in 1932 called "In Praise of Idleness" in which he argued that the glorification of busyness was one of the great moral catastrophies of industrial civilization. He was writing about factory workers. He could just as easily have been writing about knowledge workers in 2026, who carry their offices in their pockets and check email at 11pm with a feeling that is somewhere between duty and compulsion.

## What Happens When You Stop

Neuroscientists have spent the last two decades studying what the brain does when it is not doing anything in particular. The answer has turned out to be surprising and important: it does a great deal.

When you are not focused on a task, a network of brain regions called the default mode network becomes active. This is the network associated with mind-wandering, daydreaming, imagining the future, and making sense of the past. It is also associated with creativity, empathy, and the consolidation of memories.

In other words, the brain's idle state is not empty — it is busy with a different kind of work. The work of integration. Of connecting disparate experiences into something coherent. Of becoming, in a small way, more yourself.

Research by Mary Helen Immordino-Yang at the University of Southern California has shown that suppression of default mode activity — which is exactly what happens when we fill every moment with stimulation — is associated with reduced capacity for self-reflection and for understanding the emotional lives of other people. We are, in a measurable sense, less human when we are always busy.

## The Practice of Doing Nothing

Niksen is not the same as mindfulness meditation, though the two are sometimes conflated. Meditation asks you to attend carefully to the present moment. Niksen asks you not to attend to anything in particular. It is permission, not technique.

Some practitioners describe it as sitting in a chair near a window with no phone, no book, no agenda. Watching light change. Listening to sounds without naming them. Noticing thoughts without following them.

The experience, for most people, begins with discomfort. The urge to check something, do something, be somewhere else. This urge is worth sitting with. It is informative. It tells you something about the texture of your daily life and the anxiety that underlies your busyness.

After the discomfort, something else sometimes arrives. A quality of attention that is different from focused work — softer, wider, less effortful. Thoughts arise that feel genuinely surprising, as though they came from somewhere other than your deliberate mind. This is the default mode network doing its work.

## Against Optimization

There is a temptation to immediately turn niksen into a productivity hack. To point out that mind-wandering produces creative insights, that rest improves performance, that idleness is actually good for your output. All of this is true, and all of it misses the point.

The value of doing nothing is not instrumental. It is not a means to a more productive end. It is a way of remembering that you are not a productivity machine — that your value is not equivalent to your output, that your life is not a project to be optimized.

The window is there. The light is changing. You don't have to do anything with it.
