In the forests of British Columbia, ecologist Suzanne Simard has spent thirty years listening to trees talk. Not with sound — with chemistry. With carbon and water and nitrogen flowing through underground networks of fungal threads so dense that a single teaspoon of forest soil can contain miles of them. She calls it the Wood Wide Web, and it has changed how scientists think about what a forest is.

## A Forest Is Not a Collection of Trees

The conventional image of a forest is a collection of individual trees competing for light and water and space. Evolution as a war of all against all, played out in slow motion over centuries. This image is not entirely wrong — trees do compete. But it is, Simard argues, radically incomplete.

What she found, beginning with her doctoral research in the 1990s, is that trees in a forest are connected to one another through networks of mycorrhizal fungi — fungi that colonize tree roots and extend them far beyond what the roots themselves could reach. These fungal networks allow trees to exchange not just nutrients but information. When a tree is under stress from drought or insect attack, it sends chemical signals through the network. Neighboring trees respond by altering their own chemistry — producing defensive compounds before the threat reaches them.

More remarkably, Simard discovered that older, larger trees — which she calls "mother trees" — actively subsidize younger trees, including their own offspring. Carbon fixed by the mother tree's photosynthesis flows through the fungal network to seedlings growing in the shade of the forest floor, seedlings that could not otherwise produce enough energy to survive. The forest feeds its young.

## The Question of Intelligence

These findings have led some researchers, and more journalists, to ask a question that would have seemed absurd a generation ago: are trees intelligent?

It depends, of course, on what you mean by intelligent. If intelligence requires a brain, trees do not have it. But if intelligence means the ability to receive information, process it, and respond in ways that serve survival — then trees do something that looks, from a certain angle, very much like thinking.

Plant neurobiologist Stefano Mancuso has argued that plants process information in a distributed rather than centralized way. Instead of a brain, they have a kind of whole-body intelligence, with sensing and signaling happening throughout the organism. A tree's roots, for instance, can detect and respond to gravity, moisture, chemical gradients, and the presence of other roots — including the roots of kin versus strangers. Studies have shown that trees allocate more resources to siblings than to unrelated neighbors.

Monica Gagliano, a plant biologist at the University of Sydney, has gone further still, conducting experiments that she claims demonstrate something like learning in plants. In one study, she dropped potted mimosa plants repeatedly from a small height. Initially the plants folded their leaves in response — a standard defensive reaction. After repeated drops, they stopped. They had, Gagliano argues, learned that this particular disturbance was not a threat and adjusted their behavior accordingly.

These claims remain controversial. Many plant biologists think the language of intelligence and learning, applied to plants, is at best metaphorical and at worst misleading. But the skeptics and the enthusiasts agree on the underlying biology: plants are far more active, responsive, and interconnected than the passive, solitary organisms of the old textbooks.

## What This Changes

If trees communicate, cooperate, and care for their young — what does that mean for the way we manage forests?

Conventional forestry treats forests as collections of individual timber units. Clear-cutting removes not just the trees but the fungal networks that took centuries to develop. Replanting with monocultures of commercially valuable species replaces a complex, self-regulating system with something closer to a factory floor.

Simard argues that sustainable forestry requires leaving mother trees standing — not just for their seeds, but for the networks they anchor. A forest without its elders is not just less beautiful. It is, in some meaningful sense, less alive.

The wood is still there. The intelligence is gone.
