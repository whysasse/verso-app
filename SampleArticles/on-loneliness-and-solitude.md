There is a difference between loneliness and solitude that we often collapse, and the collapsing costs us something. Loneliness is an ache — the felt sense that you are not connected to others in the way you need to be. Solitude is a state — being alone, chosen or unchosen, that can hold within it either suffering or something else entirely. Confusing the two leads us to treat all aloneness as a problem to be solved, when some of it is a resource to be cultivated.

## The Epidemic Framing

Loneliness has been declared an epidemic, in the United States and elsewhere. Former Surgeon General Vivek Murthy called it a public health crisis. Studies suggest that somewhere between a quarter and a third of Americans report feeling lonely regularly, with the highest rates among young adults and the elderly. The costs are real: chronic loneliness is associated with elevated cortisol, inflammation, impaired sleep, and a mortality risk that researchers compare to smoking fifteen cigarettes a day.

None of this is in dispute. But the epidemic framing has a side effect: it pathologizes aloneness in general. It suggests that being alone is a symptom, a deficiency, a sign of social failure. And this, I think, leads us to misread our own experience in ways that make things worse.

## What Solitude Is For

The psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott wrote in 1958 about "the capacity to be alone" — which he paradoxically described as something that develops in the presence of another person. The infant learns to be alone by being alone in the company of a reliable, attentive caregiver. The capacity for solitude, in his account, is built on the foundation of secure attachment. It is a sign of psychological health, not its absence.

What Winnicott meant was that genuine solitude — solitude that nourishes rather than depletes — requires an internalized sense of connection. You have to know, at some level, that others exist and that you matter to them, in order to be comfortably alone. Loneliness, by contrast, is what happens when that internalized connection is absent or feels precarious.

This distinction has practical implications. If you are chronically lonely, the solution is connection — real, reciprocal, sustained human contact. But if what you are experiencing is simply aloneness, the prescription is different. The aloneness may be something to move toward rather than away from.

## The Texture of Good Solitude

Philosophers and writers have long described solitude as a condition for certain kinds of thinking and experience that are unavailable in company. Montaigne built a tower and retreated to it. Thoreau went to Walden. These are extreme cases, but the principle scales down.

In solitude, the performance of self that is always present in social life — even with people we love — relaxes. You stop managing how you appear and start noticing what you actually think. Ideas that were suppressed by the noise of conversation surface. Emotions that were deferred because there was no space for them find room.

The psychologist Ester Buchholz argued that solitude is not merely the absence of others but an active psychological state, as necessary for development as attachment. She called it "alonetime" and argued that our culture's discomfort with it — our rush to fill every silence with stimulation or company — reflects a deep anxiety about what we might find if we were actually quiet with ourselves.

## A Practice

None of this is to romanticize loneliness or suggest that people who are suffering from isolation should simply reframe their experience. Loneliness is painful and the pain is real.

But for those who have the basic conditions of connection met — who have relationships, who are not acutely isolated — the question of solitude is worth taking seriously. Not as a deprivation. Not as something to escape. But as a practice, like the other practices that make a life.

The question to ask is not "how do I stop being alone?" but "what do I do with the aloneness I have?" The window. The quiet. The unhurried hour. These are not problems. They are, when you can receive them as such, gifts.
